# Late-race cramping is neuromuscular fatigue, not a missing electrolyte

The folk explanation for cramping mid-race is dehydration or salt loss. The pattern that actually shows up in late-race cramping fits a different mechanism better: **sustained work above trained capacity fatigues the neuromuscular control of the muscle** — the reflex loop that balances contraction and relaxation drifts toward runaway contraction. The muscle stops being told to let go.

## How to tell them apart

The tell is **where the cramp goes next**.

- **Electrolyte/dehydration cramping** stays roughly where the work is, tracks with heat and fluid loss, and responds to replacement.
- **Neuromuscular-fatigue cramping** starts in the muscle doing the most work, then **generalises** — it spreads to muscles that weren't doing the work at all, including muscles put on stretch when you stop to stretch the first one.

If stretching one cramping muscle triggers cramp elsewhere — the back, the other leg, the torso — that is not a local electrolyte deficit. That is a system-wide loss of neuromuscular control.

## A typical picture

A runner sets out at a goal pace their training hasn't supported and holds it into the final third of the marathon. The **quads go first**; stopping to stretch them spreads cramp to the other leg, the back, the torso. The race falls apart from there. That is the classic generalising pattern.

When recording an athlete's own cramping history in [profile.md](../profile.md), capture which muscle went first, where in the race, at what pace relative to their fitness, and whether it spread. Get it from the athlete's own account; a second-hand summary easily gets both the muscle and the mechanism wrong.

## What actually fixes it

Four things, in rough order of leverage:

1. **Pace to real fitness, not target fitness.** Running above trained capacity is the primary driver. Cramping in the last 10 km while holding a pace the training didn't support is the mechanism doing exactly what it does. This is why fitness checkpoints that *re-anchor* pace matter more than checkpoints that merely confirm it — see [marathon-pace-minimal-practice](marathon-pace-minimal-practice.md).
2. **Eccentric capacity in the muscle that goes first.** For quads: squats, split squats, step-downs, and downhill-running tolerance. Eccentric loading is what degrades over marathon distance as form decays.
3. **Race-pace work late in long runs, on tired legs.** Not race-pace work when fresh. The adaptation needed is neuromuscular control *under accumulated fatigue* — see [mp-runs-inside-long-run](mp-runs-inside-long-run.md).
4. **Fuelling.** Glycogen depletion accelerates neuromuscular fatigue. Under-fuelled late-race miles arrive at the cramping threshold sooner.

Hydration and electrolytes still matter for other reasons. They are just not the lever here.

## Practical translation

Cramping in the last third of a marathon is **feedback about pacing and specific preparation**, not a nutrition failure to be solved with more salt. Treat a late-race cramp as evidence the goal pace was ahead of the fitness, and go looking at the training that was supposed to support it.

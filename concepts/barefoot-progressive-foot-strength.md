# Barefoot is a goal, not a starting point — the 3-grade footwear descent

Per Le Corre Ch 18 (Foot Freedom), conventional thick-cushioned elevated-heel sports footwear is detrimental to foot strength + sensory feedback long-term, but going directly barefoot (especially for running) is a transition error. The right approach: a **progressive 3-grade descent** from conventional → minimal → barefoot, with weeks-to-months at each grade and tissue feedback as the gate.

## Why barefoot matters

1. **Sensory feedback intensity.** ~200,000 nerve endings per sole. Conventional cushioned shoes attenuate that feedback massively — you can't sharply feel surface stability, slipperiness, hardness, temperature. The result: poorer positional adjustments, harder landings.
2. **Sensory feedback latency.** Cushioning + shoe mass adds delay between foot contact and central nervous system processing of that contact. Even ~10 ms latency degrades reactive footwork.
3. **Foot anatomy use.** 26 bones, 33 joints, ~100 muscles + tendons per foot. Encased in a "plastic cast" (Le Corre's term) of conventional shoe, most of that anatomy doesn't work. Use-it-or-lose-it applies: long-term shoe use weakens the foot.
4. **Heel elevation effects.** Most shoes have a positive heel drop (heel higher than forefoot). Forces compensatory adjustments up the chain: ankle plantarflexion bias, knee strain, hip flexor shortening, lumbar effects.
5. **Toe box constraints.** Most shoes narrow at the front, splaying the toes inward; combined with elevated rocker geometry, the foot pushes off as a unit rather than as 5 independent toes. Bunions, weak intrinsic foot muscles, lost toe-individuation.

## Why direct barefoot transition is wrong

The feet that grew up in conventional shoes are weak + sensory-attenuated. Direct switch to barefoot running adds:
- Higher impact forces (the feet adapt but slowly)
- New movement patterns (forefoot landing instead of heel)
- New surface variables (texture, temperature, contamination)
- New skin demand (no protection from abrasion, puncture, blisters)

All four at once → predictable injuries: metatarsal stress fractures, plantar fascia issues, Achilles tendinopathy, skin damage. The 2010s "born-to-run" barefoot-running wave produced these in volume.

## The 3-grade descent

| Grade | Footwear | Weeks-to-months | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **3 (conventional)** | Standard running shoe, ~8–12 mm drop, cushioned, structured | — (current state) | High-volume road running, all-day work / professional contexts |
| **2 (minimal)** | Zero-drop or low-drop (≤ 4 mm), thin sole (≤ 8 mm), flexible, wide toe box, no arch support | Months at grade 2 before considering grade 1 for running | Daily life walking, gym, easy runs, light training |
| **1 (barefoot or sock)** | Bare or thin sock | Slowly accumulate hours; outdoor terrain progression separate | Movement practice, indoor, beach, grass, prepared paths |

**Critical:** *don't skip grades*. Move down one grade at a time. Stay at each grade until it's comfortable on uneven surfaces and unconscious — not just tolerable on indoor flat floors.

## Tissue feedback as the gate

Le Corre is explicit (Ch 18): "Pay attention to the feedback from your feet as you work on adapting." The transition rate is dictated by tissue response, not by calendar.

- **Soreness day after:** acceptable if mild + transient. Persistent or escalating → back off.
- **Pain during:** stop. Reduce volume / intensity / surface complexity.
- **Skin issues:** chronic blisters = volume too high too fast; ignore the skin and you'll lose training time.
- **Achilles + calf tightness:** the most common early issue in the conventional → minimal transition (heel drop change recruits the posterior chain differently). Daily calf stretching + eccentric calf raises help.
- **Stress reaction in metatarsals:** worst-case scenario. Manifests as deep dull ache on top of the foot during + after activity. Stop, see a clinician, restart at much lower volume after weeks.

## What barefoot is NOT for

- **Long road runs.** Even after years of barefoot adaptation, asphalt for 20+ km is mechanically punishing. Minimal shoes (grade 2) are the realistic ceiling for road volume.
- **Sharp / hazardous terrain.** Glass, thorns, hot pavement, cold winter, contaminated surfaces. Use thin minimal shoes; barefoot becomes a luxury option for prepared surfaces.
- **Performance racing.** Most marathon racing benefits from some cushioning / propulsion (modern carbon-plate shoes). Barefoot racing is a niche specialisation, not a default.
- **People with diagnosed foot deformities / neuropathies.** Get clinical guidance before transitioning.

## Practical translation

Start by recording the athlete's current footwear context in [profile.md](../profile.md) (road shoes, what they lift and do calisthenics in, how much barefoot time they already get). Most runners have some barefoot indoor exposure but no structured progression.

- **During a marathon block:** *don't transition running footwear during the marathon build*. Risk-reward is terrible: a stress fracture can wipe out the A-race. Keep current road shoes for all running.
- **During a marathon block (daily life):** start the **grade 3 → grade 2 transition for daily-life walking + indoor work**. Get a pair of minimal shoes for daily wear; start with a few hours per day; build slowly. This is the only transition worth running alongside a marathon build.
- **Mobility work, any time:** do *all natural-movement practice barefoot* (crawls, balance, get-ups) on indoor flat floors. Near-zero risk.
- **Travel or a lower-volume block:** an opportunity to add outdoor barefoot exposure on safe surfaces (grass, sand, beach). Low volume, low intensity. Tests foot resilience without injury risk.
- **After the A-race:** consider grade 2 minimal shoes for *easy* runs only. Build very slowly — start with 1 km at the end of an easy run in minimal shoes; double over weeks; aim for roughly half of easy-run volume in minimal shoes after several months.
- **Permanent rule:** racing + long workouts stay in cushioned road shoes. The trade-off (foot-strength gain vs injury risk) is not worth it for a marathon goal.

# Two exercises is enough (for general physical preparation)

Per Pavel Pt I ("Simple & Sinister") + Pt II ("Why People Fail on S&S"), a well-designed minimalist strength program — Pavel's case study being **the swing + the get-up** — delivers the bulk of physical-preparation benefits for >95% of people, including most athletes. Adding exercises is the #1 way to fail the program: Pavel Macek's failure-list explicitly names "gents doing S&S with 16 kg, but adding pushups and pullups and this and that" as a cardinal sin.

## The logic

1. **The swing is the ultimate quick lift; the get-up is the ultimate slow lift.** (Bob Hoffman.) Between them they cover:
   - Explosive hip extension under load (swing)
   - Slow controlled overhead work + body-as-one-piece (get-up)
   - Posterior chain power (swing)
   - Anti-extension + anti-flexion + anti-rotation core (get-up + one-arm swing)
   - Grip + lat dominance (both)
   - Shoulder stability + packing (get-up)
   - Hip + thoracic mobility (warm-ups baked into the program)
   - Cardio + anti-glycolytic conditioning (swing)
2. **The body is a non-linear system.** Adding a third exercise does not add a third of the benefit; it slices the available recovery between three demands, often netting *less* total adaptation.
3. **Time is finite.** Multi-discipline athletes don't have budget for 60-min strength sessions 4×/week. A 25-min S&S session 2–3×/week fits.
4. **Skill consolidation requires repetition.** ([dont-work-out-practice](dont-work-out-practice.md).) Two patterns rehearsed daily beats six patterns rehearsed weekly for movement automaticity.
5. **Decision fatigue is real.** Fewer choices = more cognitive bandwidth for technique focus.

## When this principle bends

- **For competitive athletes** with sport-specific demands: still keep general-preparation strength minimalist, but add sport-specific work (a marathoner's long runs, a climber's hangboard, a fighter's sparring).
- **For advanced StrongFirst practitioners**: Pavel allows adding the snatch + military press + double-KB clean & front squat AFTER Sinister has been achieved.
- **For specific weak-link rehab**: short-term targeted work (e.g. shoulder prehab band routine) is OK alongside S&S, kept separate in time.

## When it does NOT bend

- "I want to add push-ups and pull-ups for upper-body balance" — NO. The get-up + swings train the upper body sufficiently. Add this AFTER Simple.
- "I want to add cardio HIIT for fat loss" — NO. Two-arm swings + the talk-test recovery between sets IS cardio. The lactate-flooding HIIT will damage S&S progress more than it helps.
- "I want to add stretching / yoga / mobility flow" — usually fine if separate-session, BUT Pavel explicitly warns against forward-bending stretches post-S&S (spinal flexion when fatigued).
- "I want to add abs work" — NO. The one-arm swing + get-up + bracing-behind-the-shield trains abs to "peak activation in excess of 100% MVC" (McGill's lab measurement).

## Where it conflicts with a multi-discipline plan

A plan that stacks **running + strength + other disciplines (e.g. climbing, calisthenics)** is in tension with Pavel's two-exercise principle — a marathon plan built from this vault already specifies a strength menu broader than swings + get-ups.

The resolution: **S&S is not the athlete's entire strength plan.** It's an *option* — a back-friendly minimalist substitute for the more elaborate Phase 1–3 strength sessions when life is too busy / the back is twingy / simplicity is what's needed. The full marathon-strength menu ([MOC-running-strength](../MOCs/MOC-running-strength.md)) covers more than Pavel's two lifts because:
- Calf isolation work is marathon-specific (gastroc + soleus)
- Hip thrust hits a glute-loading dose the swing doesn't reach (2× BW target per Dicharry)
- Single-leg work addresses left/right asymmetries (e.g. a one-sided hamstring issue) directly
- Carries add anti-lateral-flexion that the symmetric swing doesn't

Pavel's principle still has value: **resist the urge to keep adding to whatever the current strength session is.** If a Phase 2 session is 5 exercises, don't make it 7. Earn the right to add by owning what you have.

## The deeper principle: signal vs noise

Pavel's "two exercises" is an instance of the William of Occam principle Pavel quotes: *"It is vain to do with more what can be done with less."* The 80/20 of strength adaptation is captured by a small number of compound movements done well. The remaining 20% is bought at exponentially increasing complexity cost.

For a Type-A athlete the failure mode is collecting exercises like Pokémon — every interesting drill from every book ends up in the session. The result is a 75-minute session that does nothing brilliantly. Pavel's antidote: pick two (or five, or seven — but a small set), commit, refine.

## Quote anchor

> "It is vain to do with more what can be done with less." — William of Occam (Pavel's S&S epigraph)

> "There are only two moving parts, the swing and the get-up. No other exercises offer as many all-around benefits in such a tight package." — Pavel, *Kettlebell Simple & Sinister*, Pt I

- Paraphrase: Pavel borrows Bob Hoffman's view that building a "superman" takes both slow movements and quick lifts — the get-up is the ultimate slow lift, the swing the ultimate quick lift, so the pair covers both. (Pavel, *Kettlebell Simple & Sinister*, Pt I, pp. 9–13)

> Failure mode #9 (Pavel Macek): "Change the program or add to the program — gents doing S&S with 16kg, but adding pushups and pullups and this and that." — Pavel, *Kettlebell Simple & Sinister*, Pt II, p. 141

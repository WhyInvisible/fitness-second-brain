# No giant sets — train one exercise at a time

Sommer's explicit rejection of the circuit / WOD / giant-set pattern that dominates much of the modern bodyweight-training culture. **Train one exercise to completion. Use the integrated mobility partner as your rest. Then move to the next exercise.**

## The rule

In paraphrase: work one exercise at a time and never giant-set. Between sets, rest only as long as it takes to do that exercise's assigned integrated mobility, and finish every set and rep of the exercise before moving to the next one (Sommer, *Foundation One*, "No Giant Sets" section).

So a Sommer session looks like:
1. Warm-up (5–10 min, 21/15/9 with mastered exercises)
2. Exercise A — sets 1 → N, with `A>iM` between each set
3. Exercise B — sets 1 → N, with `B>iM` between each set
4. Exercise C — etc.
5. Done.

Not:
- Push exercise / pull exercise / leg exercise rotating × N rounds (circuit)
- A1 → A2 → A3 alternating (paired supersets, which Low actively recommends for beginners)
- Continuous high-density compound circuit (CrossFit / WOD style)

## Why no giant sets

Sommer's reasoning is implicit but follows from the other GST doctrines:

1. **Perfect practice requires undivided focus.** Cycling between exercises forces context-switching and degrades the quality of every set. Stay on one movement until it's done.
2. **The integrated mobility partner is the recovery prescription.** Inserting other strength exercises corrupts the recovery model — your mobility set is now competing with another strength set, and you're not actually mobilising the joint that needs prep.
3. **Skill-strength is brittle.** Manna, planche, lever, press handstand work needs CNS freshness on every set. The drift to slop happens when you're rushing back into a movement after a hard set of something else.
4. **Cumulative fatigue is the enemy of mastery.** The mastery-template doses are calibrated for one-exercise-at-a-time execution. Giant-setting them creates more glycolytic load than the prescription assumes.

## Contrast with Low's "paired sets cut workout time roughly in half"

Low explicitly recommends pairing opposing patterns (push + pull, push + leg) with halved rest, to cut workout time. Sommer rejects this.

Both can be right depending on the goal:
- **Low's pairing** is efficient for the *fitness-quality* outcome — strength + hypertrophy + general work capacity. For most adult intermediates, time efficiency matters and the loss of skill-strength precision is acceptable.
- **Sommer's no-giant-sets** is appropriate when *mastery of a specific skill at a specific dose* is the outcome. For elite progression, the cost of corrupted recovery is too high.

Application: during maintenance / general fitness phases (e.g. most of a marathon block), Low's pairing is fine. During focused skill progression (an off-season handstand block, planche/lever cycles), use Sommer's one-at-a-time discipline.

## The exception: warm-up

Sommer's warm-up rep schemes (21/15/9, 21/15, 15/9) using *mastered* exercises do effectively chain multiple exercises. But these are sub-mastery, moderate-RPE, and serve as primer for the working sets — not training stimulus. The no-giant-sets rule applies to the working sets only.

## Practical application

**During a marathon block:** Continue using paired push/pull sets per Low — the time efficiency matters when calisthenics is maintenance not focus.

**Off-season handstand + muscle-up block:** Apply no-giant-sets to:
- Wall handstand work (do all 3–5 sets with PPT iM between sets, before anything else)
- False-grip pull-ups / muscle-up negatives (do all sets, with lat mobility iM, before moving to dips)
- L-sit progression (do all sets with pike-position iM, before next exercise)

**Once on F1-level Sommer programming:** Strict adherence — each of the 7 fundamentals trained in sequence, with iM between every set, no overlap.

# Core stops motion; limbs create motion

Per McGill Ch 2, this is the foundational philosophy of every back-safety decision. Torso muscles and limb muscles do FUNDAMENTALLY different jobs and therefore need fundamentally different training.

## The principle

- **Limb muscles** (biceps, quads, calves, deltoids, etc.) create motion. Train through ranges of motion with load — that's what they're built for.
- **Torso muscles** (rectus abdominis, obliques, transverse abdominis, quadratus lumborum, erector spinae) primarily STOP motion. They stiffen the spine so the hips + shoulders can create motion against a stable base. Training them through ranges of motion under load (sit-ups, Russian twists, Pilates roll-up) is using the wrong job description and damages the disc.

## Consequence #1 — most core exercises in mainstream gym culture are wrong for the back

| WRONG (motion-creating) | RIGHT (motion-stopping) |
|---|---|
| Sit-ups, crunches | McGill curl-up (head barely lifts) |
| Russian twists | Pallof press, anti-rotation press |
| Pilates roll-up | Bird-dog |
| Superman (hyperextension) | Side plank |
| Bicycle crunches | Dead bug |
| Standing dumbbell side-bend | Suitcase carry |

## Consequence #2 — walking and carries train the core CORRECTLY

Every step requires the core to stop the pelvis from dropping (quadratus lumborum) and to stop spine twist (obliques). The torso doesn't shorten or lengthen — it activates to stop motion. This is why walking, suitcase carry, farmer carry, and overhead carry are core training even though they don't look like "ab work."

## Consequence #3 — power must NEVER come from the spine

Power = velocity × force. If you generate power AT the spine (the powerlifter's flexion-grind, the bodybuilder's seated rotation machine), you guarantee injury. Power generated AT the hips and shoulders, transmitted THROUGH a stiff torso, is what athletes call "athleticism" — and what spares the back.

## Application

This principle, internalised, is the silent guardrail behind every training decision in a back-conscious plan:
- Why the McGill Big 3 (motion-stopping) are daily; sit-ups never appear.
- Why the trap-bar deadlift substitute works (hip drive with locked spine vs barbell deadlift's higher shear).
- Why the carries (suitcase, farmer, overhead) belong in the upper-body menu — they're core work, not arms work.
- Why a stronger glute drive matters more than a stronger lower back.
- Why the front lever progression (anti-extension core) is good, and the V-up (sit-up motion) is bad.

## Cross-source check

Dicharry independently arrives at the same conclusion: "Your six-pack is for show, not for go." Both authors converge on dynamic anti-rotation + anti-extension + anti-lateral-flexion as the core training menu. The McGill version is more conservative (isometric base first, dynamic later); Dicharry's is more performance-oriented (layers dynamic earlier). For an athlete with a back-injury history (see [profile.md](../profile.md)): McGill order wins — Big 3 isometric capacity first, Dicharry dynamic later.

# Train fast to be fast

Per McGill UBF Ch 13-14:

> "Train slow to be slow, train fast to be fast." (UBF Ch 13, attributed to Vasily Alexeyev, Olympic super-heavyweight champion.)

> "Train as fast as one can — not as fast as one can't." (UBF Ch 14.)

> "Most NFL linemen will tell you that if you are squatting slowly then you have too heavy a load." (UBF Ch 13.)

The single most under-respected principle in popular strength training. Velocity of contraction is itself a training variable, INDEPENDENT of load. Fast contractions recruit motor units that slow contractions never reach — and you can't develop those motor units by training slowly.

## The core mechanism

Carlo Deluca's EMG research (cited in UBF Ch 14): different velocities of contraction recruit different motor units. The high-threshold, fast-twitch motor units required for athletic power are ONLY recruited by:
1. Very high loads (maximal effort, 1-2 reps), AND/OR
2. **Fast/explosive contractions** (intent of maximum velocity, even if actual velocity is moderate).

Slow contractions with moderate loads recruit only low-threshold, slow-twitch motor units. You can do 3 sets of 10 at 70% with control for years and never train the motor units that matter for athletic performance.

## Behm & Sale (1993) — the intent matters

Cited in UBF Ch 13: **the INTENT to move as fast as possible recruits high-threshold motor units, EVEN IF the actual velocity is not maximal.**

This means: a heavy deadlift performed with the INTENT of explosive speed (even if the bar moves slowly because the weight is heavy) recruits more motor units than the same weight lifted "in control."

## The implication for "heavy/light" vs "fast/slow" session design

McGill's point (UBF Ch 14, p. 276, paraphrased): so-called light sessions usually just exhaust the athlete and reflect a bodybuilding mindset, not a performance one. Performance is more likely to improve with a "fast" session / "slow" session split.

- **Slow session:** large resistance, slower contraction — builds neuromuscular capacity for dense contraction (raw strength).
- **Fast session:** lighter weight, MAXIMAL VELOCITY intent — trains the motor units that produce athletic power.

The bodybuilding "heavy day + light recovery day" model misses this. A "light" day is usually higher-volume, lower-velocity, fatigue-inducing — the opposite of what speed-strength training needs.

## The deadlift example (UBF Ch 14, p. 267-268)

UBF's specific recommendation for the deadlift (paraphrased): because the lift is so slow, the deadlift isn't really a power movement. Powerlifters who train slowly near their max often stall; many progress better with true power training — e.g. dropping the weight and lifting much faster, which recruits a different population of motor units.

This is the model for an athlete with back history who wants to return to a conventional deadlift pattern (per [return-to-sport-progression-after-back-injury](return-to-sport-progression-after-back-injury.md)):
1. Lower the weight.
2. Raise the bar onto blocks (knee height).
3. Lift at the **fastest rate possible**.
4. Spine locked, glutes dominant.
5. Stop before fatigue (the explosive set has 2-3 reps, not 8-10).

The "block-pull at speed" model is the safe re-entry — explicitly NOT slow-heavy grinding.

## How this differs from bar-speed-rule (Dicharry)

[Bar-speed-rule](bar-speed-rule.md) from Dicharry: keep bar velocity >50% of max — if it drops below, the weight is too heavy or you're fatigued; STOP the set. The rule prevents grinding through rep-quality failures.

UBF's "train fast to be fast" goes further: deliberately TRAIN at speed (not just monitor that speed doesn't drop). Pick weights that ALLOW fast execution as the goal, not just as a safety margin.

**Synthesis:** Dicharry = floor (never below 50% bar speed). UBF = ceiling (deliberately train AT maximum velocity intent). Both are correct + complementary.

## Application: programming

**During a marathon block:** slow-controlled, moderate-heavy strength work is appropriate for capacity building, because high-CNS-demand speed work would compete with running.

**Off-season (after the A-race):**
- Add explicit "fast" sessions to the strength rotation.
- Block-pull deadlifts at speed (light weight, 2-3 reps, max-velocity intent).
- Speed-squats with bands or chains (Westside-style).
- Plyometric work (per UBF Ch 14: hops on the square, lateral box hops, medicine-ball tosses).
- Kettlebell swings as the daily speed-strength tool (Pavel S&S = ideal post-marathon programming for this).

**Always relevant:**
- The "intent of maximum velocity" cue applies to EVERY rep, not just speed days. Even a hypertrophy set at 75% benefits from the intent to move the concentric explosively (per Bar Speed Rule + Behm & Sale).

## Why this matters for the marathon

For pure marathon running, "train fast to be fast" applies to:
- **Strides** (4-8 × 20-30s at near-maximum, full recovery) — pure speed-strength, low fatigue.
- **Short hill sprints** (per Pfitzinger: 10-12 s reps) — explosive force production, recruits high-threshold motor units.
- **Reps work** (per Daniels: R pace, 200-400 m at very fast pace, full recovery).

These are LOW-VOLUME high-INTENSITY workouts that recruit the high-threshold motor units even within an endurance-dominated training plan. Keep them in the program every week of the base + sharpening phases — they're not just speed work, they're motor-unit-pool preservation.

## What this is NOT

- NOT "lift maximally every session" — that's the failure mode. Speed-strength is high-quality, low-volume, max-RECOVERY work.
- NOT "always train fast" — the slow-heavy days build the raw capacity that speed days then convert to power.
- NOT a license to compromise form for speed. UBF Ch 14 is explicit that the target speed is the fastest one at which technique stays excellent — never fast with movement flaws (paraphrased).

## Cross-source notes

- Pavel: hardstyle "one strike one kill" = EVERY swing/rep is performed at maximum intent, never grinding. The S&S program IS train-fast-to-be-fast applied to two exercises.
- Daniels: R-pace + strides = speed training within a marathon plan. The 6-second rule between zones ensures slow work doesn't pollute speed work.
- Pfitzinger: short hill sprints + strides serve the same function for runners.
- Matthews: heavy-slow 4-6 rep approach is the OPPOSITE end — appropriate for hypertrophy/raw-strength goals, but should be paired with speed work for performance transfer.

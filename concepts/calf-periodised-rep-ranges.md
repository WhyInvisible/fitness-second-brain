# Calf training: periodised rep ranges across the week

Per Matthews Ch 17. Calves are an outlier muscle group — they recover faster than larger muscles and respond particularly well to periodised rep ranges within the SAME training week. The protocol is 3 sessions per week, each at a different rep range and rest interval.

## The protocol

3× per week, at least 1 day between sessions. Within each session: rotate toe position across sets (forward → out 20° → in 20° → repeat) to vary fibre recruitment.

### Workout A — heavy
- Standing calf raise: 3 × 4-6 reps
- Seated calf raise: 3 × 4-6 reps
- Rest 2-3 min between sets

### Workout B — moderate
- Leg press calf raise: 3 × 8-10 reps
- Donkey calf raise (or another leg press calf): 3 × 8-10 reps
- Rest 1-2 min between sets

### Workout C — light / endurance
- Standing calf raise: 3 × 12-15 reps
- Seated calf raise: 3 × 12-15 reps
- Rest 1 min between sets

Matthews' example schedule: Monday A, Wednesday B, Friday C.

## Why periodise within the week

- Calves are composed of high proportions of Type I (slow-twitch) fibres compared to other major muscles — they handle the all-day standing, walking, running we throw at them
- Type I responds to higher-rep work; Type II responds to lower-rep heavy work
- Hitting BOTH adaptations across the week stimulates more total growth than just one
- Recovery is fast enough to handle 3 sessions in 5 days without overtraining

## Form — non-negotiable for any rep range

- **Bottom of rep:** heels as low as possible, deep stretch in calves
- **Top of rep:** full ballerina tippy-toe — ankle joint fully plantarflexed
- **Tempo:** 2-1-2 (2 s up, 1 s pause at top, 2 s down)
- Reducing ROM ("just bouncing at the bottom") is the most common reason calves don't grow

## Why this matters for marathon runners

Marathon training puts MASSIVE volume on calves (every running step = calf contraction × 30,000+ in a long run). Yet many runners do no calf-strength work at all — check the athlete's current routine in [profile.md](../profile.md). Calf strength is the difference between:
- Calf strain or cramp at km 30+ (the classic late-race failure) vs. ability to push off through km 42
- Achilles tendinopathy risk vs. resilient lower legs
- Soleus stiffness limiting stride vs. powerful push-off

The protocol scales down for marathon-block use:
- **Phase 1-2 (marathon rehab/build):** 1-2× per week single-leg calf raise (BW, slow eccentric) — already in [MOC-running-strength.md](../MOCs/MOC-running-strength.md)
- **Phase 4 (progressive loading):** can add Workout A (heavy) on the heavy lift day + Workout B (moderate) on a separate day
- **Phase 5 (maintenance):** drop to 1× per week of Workout B
- **Post-A-race / off-season block:** full 3× per week A/B/C rotation as Matthews prescribes

## Single-leg always preferred over double for runners

Matthews implicitly does double-leg. For runners, Dicharry is right: running is a series of SINGLE-LEG actions, so single-leg calf raises transfer better. Use double-leg loaded variants only when single-leg has progressed past available loading (e.g., bodyweight single-leg × 25 reps is the prerequisite to loaded double-leg).

## Soleus matters as much as gastrocnemius for runners

- **Seated calf raise** isolates the soleus (knee bent ~90° takes gastroc out)
- **Standing calf raise** hits gastroc more (knee straight loads two-joint muscle)
- Distance running depends heavily on soleus (the deep, slow-twitch endurance calf muscle)
- Sprinting / jumping depends more on gastroc (the showier, fast-twitch surface calf)
- Marathon training = prioritise soleus = seated calf raise gets more attention than Matthews would naturally give

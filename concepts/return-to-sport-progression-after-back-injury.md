# Return-to-sport progression after back injury (McGill's 5-stage roadmap)

Per McGill UBF Ch 9-14 + the case studies in Ch 16. The structured path from "back is pain-free" to "back can perform athletically again." The standard sequence McGill applies to every athlete he sees, customised to the sport.

## The 5-stage framework (mandatory order)

> "No clinician, or therapy approach, will be entirely successful without removing the cause of the back troubles." — UBF Preface

Stages cannot be skipped. Each stage's competencies are PREREQUISITES for the next.

| Stage | Duration | Goal | Test of completion |
|---|---|---|---|
| **0. Cause identification + removal** | Days-weeks | Find the mechanical trigger; eliminate it from daily life | Pain-free for ≥2 consecutive weeks |
| **1. Motor patterns** | 2-6+ weeks | Spine position awareness, hip-hinge, ab brace, glute reactivation, lock rib-cage-on-pelvis | Can perform unloaded squat, hinge, lunge, carry with zero spine motion + glute dominance |
| **2. Stability** | 4-12 weeks | Big 3 progressed to advanced forms (rolling side bridge, sweep+squares bird-dog, pre-braced curl-up) | Big 3 advanced forms held under heavy breathing without form break |
| **3. Endurance** | Concurrent with Stage 2 | Russian Reverse Pyramid for Big 3; asymmetric carries (QL); crocodile breathing | Side bridge full version held while breathing through a straw; suitcase carry 30+ s without lateral lean |
| **4. Strength** | 8-16 weeks | Surround the dragon — train every joint above/below the spine; sagittal then frontal then torsional | Loaded hinge, push, pull, carry, lunge at reasonable sport-specific weight with no compensations |
| **5. Speed / power / sport-specific** | Continual | Block-pulls at speed, plyometrics, sport-specific drills with super-stiffness cues at moments of impact | Sport performance restored to or beyond pre-injury level |

## What this is NOT

- **NOT a 6-week timeline.** Tissue heals over years ([back-tissue-heals-over-years](back-tissue-heals-over-years.md)). Performance restoration takes the time it takes.
- **NOT additive on top of unaddressed cause.** If the cause (flexion-intolerance, glute amnesia, hip stiffness, dysfunctional motor pattern) is still in place, all 5 stages just paper over it. Cause first.
- **NOT generic.** Each stage's exercises are customised to the athlete's sport, history, and current limits. UBF's case studies in Ch 16 are 14 worked examples of the same skeleton applied differently.

## Practical application: placing the athlete, and the commonly skipped Stage 1

Use the athlete's back history in [profile.md](../profile.md) → Injuries and niggles to place them on the table above. A common pattern in athletes who rebuilt on their own, years after an injury and now pain-free:
- Stage 0 done (the cause, e.g. heavy conventional deadlifts, removed).
- Stages 2-3 done implicitly via general fitness + daily Big 3.
- Stage 1's explicit glute reactivation skipped. A one-sided hamstring asymmetry, or hinges that feel hamstring- and lower-back-dominant, is the classic [glute-amnesia](glute-amnesia.md) signal.
- Already at Stage 4 (loaded hinges via trap-bar, single-leg deadlift) but possibly reinforcing dysfunctional patterns (hamstring + erector spinae dominance) instead of glute-dominant ones.

**Implication:** backfill Stage 1's glute reactivation before continuing to load Stage 4 patterns — not as full rehab, but as a 5-min pre-leg-day protocol (see [glute-amnesia](glute-amnesia.md)). This is high-leverage, low-cost.

## Practical application: the conventional deadlift question

An athlete with a deadlift-related back injury is often told "trap-bar permanently." If they ever want to re-attempt the conventional deadlift pattern (an off-season project, never during a marathon build), UBF gives a road map. From the powerlifter case study (UBF Ch 16, pp. 296-298):

1. Confirm hip + ankle flexibility (potty-squat test).
2. Stages 1-3 fully owned (glute reactivation included).
3. **Unloaded bar.** Inhale → fill thorax + abdomen → tension abs + back + lats together → grip bar → neutral or slight extension → groove the motion.
4. **No belt.** Belts are for competition.
5. Groove unloaded for weeks-months.
6. **Block-pull deadlifts** (bar raised to knee height) at HIGH SPEED with LIGHT weight — different motor unit pool than slow heavy lifts; lower spine load; speed builds the explosive pattern. Train FAST to be fast, not slow with heavy.
7. Slowly lower the blocks (over months).
8. Two "lifting cams" simultaneously: hip ER (image "spread the floor") + lat engagement (image "bend the bar" via shoulder ER).
9. **Never sacrifice form for load.** Year after year of athletes ruining their backs by not heeding this — UBF Ch 13.

**The block-pull-at-speed model is the key insight.** Slow heavy grinding is the opposite of what re-introduces the deadlift safely. Light + fast + perfect form first.

## The marathon-runner specific application (UBF Ch 16, p. 300)

UBF's marathon-runner case study is the case most directly relevant to runners. Key adjustments to the 5-stage framework for runners:

- **Stage 1:** add running gait screen — look for asymmetric lumbar torsion at foot strike. If present (often a glute med + QL issue on the opposite side), address before adding mileage.
- **Stage 2:** Big 3 includes **side bridge under heavy breathing** as a specific running-relevant skill (lateral stability during gait).
- **Stage 3:** **suitcase carry for QL endurance** is the most running-transferable Stage 3 exercise.
- **Stage 4:** "Surround the dragon" means hip flexion power (sprinters use psoas-loaded patterns; marathoners use it less but still need basic capacity) + foot/ankle strength for stride mechanics.
- **Stage 5:** for marathoners the "power" stage is less plyometric than for sprinters; more about strides + hill repeats + cadence work. The "no joint between shoulders and hips" cue stays for every run.

## Mantras from UBF that govern the whole progression

(McGill, *Ultimate Back Fitness*.)

> "Only add more load when your spine is in a neutral posture that is possible to maintain."

> "Never sacrifice exercise form to lift or move more load."

> "What you don't do is just as important, if not more, as what you do do."

> "Pain during the rehabilitation process impedes the establishment of healthy motion and motor patterns and pain is usually the result of a poor program."

# Russian Descending Pyramid (the McGill Big 3 dosing system)

Per McGill Ch 9 (p. 103). The set/rep scheme McGill uses for all three Big 3 exercises (and which McGill attributes to Russian strength masters). Built to optimise endurance gain while preventing the muscle cramping and form breakdown that re-injures the back.

## The basic structure

1 rep = 1 hold of 8–10 seconds.

- **Set 1:** 6 reps, rest 20–30 s
- **Set 2:** 4 reps, rest 20 s
- **Set 3:** 2 reps. Finished.

Then move to the next Big 3 exercise and repeat.

For side plank and bird-dog, run the pyramid per side (so set 1 = 6 reps right + 6 reps left, etc.).

## Why descending (not ascending or constant)

- The 8–10 s hold restores the muscle's O₂ / acid balance between reps, preventing the cramping that destroys form. **UBF Ch 12 quantifies this:** near-infrared spectroscopy shows torso muscles deplete oxygen rapidly at the contraction levels used in the Big 3 — the rest interval restores it. Hold longer than 8-10 s and you cross into oxygen-starvation territory where form breaks.
- Descending reps means the heaviest set (most reps) is when you're freshest. Subsequent sets work the muscle at decreasing load as it fatigues — letting endurance accumulate without driving it into the failure zone where form fails.
- Total volume across the three sets (6+4+2 = 12 reps × 8–10 s = ~100 s under tension) is meaningful but recoverable.

## UBF's variant — the 5/4/3 endurance pyramid (Ch 12)

UBF (Ch 12, p. 227) presents the same descending pyramid pattern with a slightly different example: **5 right / 5 left / REST / 4 right / 4 left / REST / 3 right / 3 left.** Same principle (descending reps, fatigue tolerance protected by reducing reps as the muscle tires), slightly different numbers. Both are valid. The PATTERN matters more than the exact starting reps.

UBF (Ch 12, p. 227) also clarifies the long-term progression: **only once the pyramid is well practised and fatigue never compromises technique can the hold time per rep be lengthened, and even then only if wanted.** So the eventual ceiling allows longer holds, but only AFTER years of pyramid practice with zero form breakdown.

## The progression rule (counterintuitive)

When the dose feels easy: **add ONE rep to each set. Do NOT extend the hold time.**

So:
- Beginner: 6/4/2
- Earned next step: 7/5/3
- Earned again: 8/6/4
- ...etc.

Why: longer holds → more cramping risk → form breaks down → injury. More reps at the same short hold → more endurance work, same form quality.

## Why this differs from gym dosing

Strength gym work (e.g. [bar-speed-rule](bar-speed-rule.md), [runner-strength-loading-targets](runner-strength-loading-targets.md)) uses heavier weight + lower reps to build force production. The McGill Big 3 is the OPPOSITE goal — build endurance for daily stability without taxing the spine. The pyramid is engineered for that goal:
- No load (bodyweight only)
- Isometric (no spine motion)
- Short holds (no cramping)
- Multiple sets (endurance volume)
- Descending (sustainable across daily practice)

## How to split if too much

If the full Big 3 pyramid in one session is too much (acute pain phase, very tender back), split:
- **Morning:** curl-up pyramid
- **Afternoon:** side plank pyramid
- **Evening:** bird-dog pyramid

Or do half-pyramids twice per day. The total work matters more than the timing.

## When to STOP a session

Form breaks. Cramping starts. Pain in the back (not muscle burn). Any of these = end the exercise. The next day, regress one rep step.

## Practical translation

A common default Big 3 dosing looks like "3 × 8 s curl-up holds, 2 × 20 s side plank each side, 2 × 8 bird dog per side" (compare [MOCs/MOC-core-training.md](../MOCs/MOC-core-training.md)). McGill's pyramid is mechanically a small change but conceptually a real upgrade:
- Holds at 8–10 s (a 20 s side plank is too long per McGill's cramping argument).
- 6/4/2 instead of 3 × 8 s or 2 × 20 s.
- Progression: add reps (e.g. 7/5/3), don't extend holds.

For athletes inclined to do "more" because they assume more helps, this is the bedrock anti-overdoing rule.

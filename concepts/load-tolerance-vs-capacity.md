# Load tolerance vs capacity (the dose-management framework)

Per McGill UBF Ch 1, two distinct dose-related concepts that athletes routinely confuse, with predictable injury consequences.

## The two concepts

**Tolerance** = the load above which TISSUES BREAK. A red LINE, not to be crossed. Lower in injured tissue. Can be raised by training over time.

**Capacity** = the total amount of work the system can do per session, day, or week. A BUDGET that gets spent. Finite per period.

The two are independent:
- A high-tolerance athlete (well-trained tissues, safe under heavy load) can still BLOW capacity by overdoing volume (training drained, daily life loads add up, the system has nothing left for life or the next session).
- A low-capacity athlete (sleep-deprived, marathon-training, stressed) may have plenty of tolerance margin but no SPARE capacity, so a normal load becomes a breaking load because there's no recovery left.

## Why athletes mismanage both

**Tolerance mismanagement:**
- Pushing into territory the tissues can't handle (the classic ego-lift, the rapid jump in load, the "PR Friday no matter what").
- For back-injured athletes: tolerance is permanently below pre-injury. Adding pre-injury loads invites re-injury.

**Capacity mismanagement (UBF's specific contribution):**
- **Wasted on warmup.** Long, heavy warmups → form breaks in the main work because capacity was spent. UBF Ch 1: "some use up too much capacity in warmup activities, causing them to break form in the actual activity."
- **Wasted on bad daily posture.** Chronic chin-poke, slouched sitting → erectors fire ALL DAY for nothing. By gym time the lower back is already 20% drained. UBF Ch 1 (paraphrased): others burn capacity through poor standing and sitting posture, setting the back up for pain and dysfunction in the session itself and in the recovery afterwards.
- **Wasted on bodybuilding isolation.** Chasing pump and aesthetic with high-volume isolation work uses capacity that should go to performance training.

## "Ideal training occurs when both tolerance and capacity are increased, together with perfecting form and ultimately performance." (UBF Ch 1)

The good plan grows tolerance (carefully, over time, with the [10%/week](progressive-overload.md) rule) AND respects capacity (programs to the budget available this week, not the budget you'd have if you were fresh).

## Application: marathon training is a high-capacity drain

A multi-discipline athlete in a marathon build is the textbook capacity-management problem:
- Marathon training = 50-70+ km/week running + long runs that drain for 2-3 days post-session.
- Strength, calisthenics or other sports (see [goals.md](../goals.md)) on top.
- Daily life capacity also matters (work, sleep, stress).
- If [profile.md](../profile.md) records a back injury, that tissue is healing with permanently reduced tolerance.

**Implications:**
1. **Strength volume during peak marathon block must be LOWER than off-season.** Same exercises, fewer sets, longer rest, never to failure. The body's capacity is mostly going to running adaptation.
2. **Warmup must be efficient.** 10 min that wakes the body up, not 30 min that drains it.
3. **Daily posture matters.** A workday of chin-poke + slouched sitting = 6-8 hours of low-grade erector contraction = baseline capacity already half-spent before training. Spine hygiene ([spine-sparing-daily-life](spine-sparing-daily-life.md)) is not just back-safety — it's capacity preservation.
4. **Recovery (sleep, walking, easy nutrition) is capacity restoration.** Sleep < 7 hours = capacity halved next day.
5. **Cutback weeks are not optional.** Every 3-4 weeks ([cutbacks-every-3-4-weeks](cutbacks-every-3-4-weeks.md)) reset the tolerance + capacity ledger.

## Diagnostic questions before each session

1. **How much capacity did I have when I woke up?** (Sleep, stress, soreness, mood — see [listen-to-morning-signals](listen-to-morning-signals.md).)
2. **How much have I already spent today?** (Work day intense? Long meeting? Already walked 5 km?)
3. **How much will this session cost?** (Easy run = low cost. Threshold + leg day same day = stacked cost.)
4. **How much do I need to keep in reserve for tomorrow + recovery?** (Tomorrow is a long run? Today's strength must be deliberately sub-optimal.)

If the math doesn't work, the session changes — drop intensity, drop volume, or skip.

## The race-week implication

Race week: capacity must be fully restored. This means:
- Drop strength volume to maintenance (1 short session, sub-failure).
- Maintain run volume slightly reduced; intensity to MP-confirm-only.
- Sleep priority.
- Daily-posture vigilance.
- McGill Big 3 stays (low capacity cost, high readiness value).
- Anything not capacity-restoring or race-prepping is OUT.

## The injury-recovery implication

If a niggle appears: tolerance has been temporarily lowered. The right response is NOT "rest a week then resume normal load" — it's:
1. Drop training load to BELOW current (lowered) tolerance.
2. Rebuild capacity through gentle work (walking, Big 3, easy mobility).
3. Slowly raise load again, watching for tolerance signals.

This is what [virtual-surgery-relapse-protocol](virtual-surgery-relapse-protocol.md) operationalises for the back specifically.

## Cross-source consistency

- Pfitzinger: the periodisation logic IS tolerance + capacity management at the seasonal scale.
- Daniels: the weekly caps on T (10%) + I (8%) + R (5%) are capacity ceilings; the VDOT system is a tolerance calibration tool.
- Pavel: the "anti-glycolytic / talk-test rest" model + "don't work out, practice" = pure capacity preservation. Pavel's program respects capacity ruthlessly.
- Matthews: deload every 8-10 weeks = capacity reset; double-progression = tolerance growth in controlled steps.

UBF's contribution is to NAME the two concepts as distinct and to point out the specific capacity-waste patterns (warmup, posture, isolation).

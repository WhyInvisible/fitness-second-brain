# Active vs passive flexibility (and stability)

Low's clean three-way distinction, which sharpens Dicharry's flexibility-vs-mobility split:

- **Passive flexibility** — a joint can be moved (by you, by a partner, by gravity) to some range of motion. "Touch your toes" is passive flexibility (gravity pulls you down).
- **Active flexibility / mobility** — you can **actively** put yourself in that position using your own muscles. Pike compression where you pull your chest to your thighs *using abs/hip flexors* is active flexibility.
- **Stability** — neuromuscular control of the position once you're there. Holding the pike position without wobbling = stability.

A flexible body that can't actively get into its range is largely useless for athletic skill. An active range with no stability collapses under load.

## Why this matters for calisthenics

Many skills look like they're "flexibility limited" but are actually active-flexibility limited:

- **L-sit:** needs hamstring length (passive) AND ability to actively pull knees toward chest with abs/hip flexors (active). Most people have the passive but not the active.
- **Press handstand:** needs deep pike (chest to thighs) actively, not just passively. The press requires actively pulling into compression while pushing through the floor.
- **Manna:** the manna is the *active* end-range of shoulder extension. You can't passively be helped into a manna; the shoulder has to *generate force* at the end-range.
- **Front lever:** the body must be actively held parallel to ground; passive flexibility of the shoulders is irrelevant.

## Training all three

| Type | How to train | Frequency |
|---|---|---|
| Passive flexibility | Static stretching at end of workout, 30–60 s per stretch, into discomfort not pain. PNF (contract-relax) for chronic tightness. Loaded stretching for advanced. | Daily if a body part is currently the limiter |
| Active flexibility / mobility | Active range-of-motion work — pike compression, straddle compression, shoulder dislocates with band, end-range isometric holds | Daily mobility, 5–10 min |
| Stability | Single-leg balances, planks, hollow holds, controlled end-range movement (e.g. slow eccentric lowering into bottom of squat) | 2–3×/wk integrated into warm-up |

## The rule of thumb

If a skill is hard, ask **in this order**:
1. Do I have the passive range? (Can someone else move me there?)
2. Can I actively get there myself? (Without help, can I move into the position?)
3. Can I hold there with control? (No wobbling, no compensation pattern)

Whichever is the limiter is the training target.

## Application

During a marathon build (or any phase where another discipline is the A-goal in [goals.md](../goals.md)): the **active end** of this principle applies — daily mobility maintenance is non-negotiable and cheap. Don't invest in deep passive flexibility work (splits, manna preparation) — save that for an off-season or calisthenics-focused block.

When calisthenics becomes the primary focus: active flexibility training becomes a major investment. Specifically:
- **Pike compression + hamstring length** — gate for L-sit upgrades + front lever + press handstand
- **Straddle compression + adductor length** — gate for straddle planche + straddle front lever + manna
- **Shoulder extension (German hang)** — gate for back lever + manna
- **Wrist flexion** — gate for false grip + handstand volume

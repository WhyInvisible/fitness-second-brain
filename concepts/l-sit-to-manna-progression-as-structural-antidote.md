# L-sit → manna: the single most under-trained antidote to calisthenics imbalance

Steven Low's strongest structural-balance recommendation: **pair handstand with the L-sit → V-sit → manna progression**. The pairing single-handedly trains:

- **Vertical push** (handstand) + **vertical pull** (the entire manna progression loads scapular retractors + posterior delts + external rotators)
- **Overhead flexion** (handstand) + **shoulder extension end-range** (manna pushes shoulders to their hyperextension limit)
- **Anterior shoulder** (handstand) + **posterior shoulder** (manna)
- **Compression** as a bonus (manna progression requires deep hamstring/hip compression — useful for front lever, press handstand, anything advanced)

This is why Low describes the pairing as the "perfect couple" for self-managing structural balance.

## Why the posterior-shoulder + scap-retractor + external-rotator combo matters

These are exactly the muscles that get under-loaded in any push-dominant program (which most calisthenics programmes are by default — dips, pushups, planche progressions, handstand pushups all hammer the anterior shoulder). Imbalance leads to:

- Forward shoulder posture
- Anterior impingement
- Labrum wear (long-term)
- Force-output inhibition (the body limits force when it senses instability)

Most programs deal with this by stacking horizontal rows + face pulls + scap retraction work. **One L-sit-to-manna progression replaces all of that.**

## The level progression (Low's numbers)

| Level | Skill | Target |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Tuck L-sit | 3 × 20 s |
| 2 | One-leg-bent L-sit (alternating) | 3 × 15 s each |
| 3 | **L-sit** | 3 × 20 s |
| 4 | Straddle L-sit | 3 × 15 s |
| 5 | Rings-turned-out L-sit | 3 × 15 s |
| 6 | 45-degree V-sit | 3 × 5 s |
| 7 | 75-degree V-sit | 3 × 5 s |
| 8 | 100-degree V-sit | 3 × 5 s |
| 9–12 | 120 → 170-degree V-sit | progressively |
| 13 | **Manna** | hold |

## The "hands backward" cue

For everything from straddle L-sit onwards: **hands point backward** (fingers away from body, toward your feet). This puts you in the position needed for the manna eventually and starts loading posterior shoulder right away. Beginners often place hands forward — easier short-term, but doesn't build the manna pathway.

## Compression: the missing piece

L-sits aren't just about supporting your weight on locked arms. The **compression** (pulling the legs up toward the chest using abs + hip flexors + quads + hamstring flexibility) is the rate-limiter for everything from L-sit upgrades to front lever to press handstand.

**Compression-specific work** to add alongside L-sits:
- Seated, legs straight in front, lift heels off floor — 3 × 10
- Seated pike compression with hands behind: lift legs straight off floor as high as possible, 3 × 10 s
- Pike stretch (chest toward thighs) — daily

## Active when?

- **Maintenance (e.g. during a marathon build):** tuck L-sit 3 × 20 s once a week is cheap and worthwhile. Keeps the motor pattern alive.
- **Calisthenics-primary block:** the full progression (L-sit → straddle → V-sit) is one of the four key skill ladders in the first 6-month block, alongside handstand, muscle-up, front lever (tuck).

## Why this is "the antidote"

Most calisthenics writers tell you to do face pulls + scapular retractions + reverse flies for posterior shoulder balance. Low's insight: the L-sit-to-manna progression covers all of that in one skill ladder, AND it builds compression, AND it's an impressive skill goal in its own right. Triple-duty work.

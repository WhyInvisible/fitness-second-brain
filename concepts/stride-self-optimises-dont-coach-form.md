# The stride self-optimises — don't coach form

Fitzgerald (channeling Lydiard, supported by 40+ years of biomechanics research): **forced "corrections" to a runner's natural stride almost always worsen running economy.** The stride self-optimises through high-volume running, and conscious meddling is counterproductive.

## The evidence

- **Cavanagh 1982** — 10 runners forced to run at stride lengths ±20% of their natural length: every single runner was most economical at their *own* natural length. Any imposed change cost efficiency.
- **Halvorsen 2012 (Sweden)** — 16 male runners given feedback to reduce vertical bouncing. They succeeded in reducing it. They became *less* efficient.
- **Heel-strike vs forefoot-strike** — runners forced to switch from natural heel-strike to forefoot strike were still less economical 12 weeks later (Cape Town 2005).
- **Self-optimisation in newbies** — Dixon 2012: 10 beginner female runners trained for 10 weeks with no form coaching. Running economy improved 8.4% via 7 subtle, unconscious stride changes (e.g., timing of peak ankle dorsiflexion in the stance phase) — adjustments no one could have consciously instructed.

## Why it works

1. **The stride is a self-optimising system.** Each stride is slightly different (the brain literally cannot create the same motor plan twice). Variations that produce equal speed with less muscle activation get "selected" by the brain and reinforced. With enough repetition, efficiency emerges.
2. **A quiet brain produces a freer stride.** Experienced runners exhibit *more* stride variability than beginners, not less — a sign of loose neural control. Less micromanaging brain → more adaptive stride → better economy.
3. **Conscious attention to form costs efficiency.** A 2009 German study found runners were less economical when thinking about body movements than when focusing on breathing, and less economical focusing on breathing than focusing on the environment. The biomechanics didn't change — only the brain activity did.

## The RunBot analogy

Early walking robots were preprogrammed with rigid "perfect form" — they could only do what they were told and looked stiff and lifelike. RunBot (2008) used a few simple reflex rules and let the body's sensors handle adaptation. Result: more graceful, more adaptive, learned to walk slopes it wasn't programmed for. **Looseness > rigid control.** Same applies to humans.

## What this means practically

- **Stop trying to fix your foot strike, cadence, posture, etc. consciously during the run.** It backfires.
- **Run more, easy, often.** Volume + repetition = self-optimisation. ~80% easy lets you accumulate the strides without burning out.
- **Don't pay technique coaches for form drills.** Save the money.

## How to reconcile with Dicharry

[body-drives-form](body-drives-form.md) (Dicharry's "your body drives your form") says: most form problems are *capacity* problems — tight hips, weak glutes, locked T-spine. The fix is mobility + strength to enable better form, *not* cueing better form.

Fitzgerald agrees from the other direction: don't drill the form, let the body sort it out. The two are compatible:
- **Dicharry:** fix the physical prerequisites (off the run).
- **Fitzgerald:** during the run, run and let the stride emerge.

Together: build the body in the gym + mobility work; then run a lot, easy, and trust the brain to find the optimum.

## Where Fitzgerald might be wrong

The Dicharry-Fitzgerald reconciliation has a soft spot: some elite-level form cues (e.g., "land under your centre of mass," cadence ≥170) demonstrably help runners who have specific dysfunctions. The blanket "don't coach form" is a useful corrective to over-coaching, but a few targeted cues from a knowledgeable coach can short-circuit years of trial-and-error self-optimisation. Use sparingly.

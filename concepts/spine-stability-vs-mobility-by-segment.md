# Spine stability vs mobility by spinal segment

Back Mechanic gave the coarse rule: "spine = stiffness, hips and shoulders = mobility." UBF refines this by spinal segment — different parts of the spine want different things, and treating the spine as one undifferentiated structure misses important distinctions.

## The segment-by-segment breakdown

| Segment | Wants | Why | Training implication |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Cervical (neck)** | Stability under athletic load, modest mobility at rest | Chronic chin-poke / forward head wastes capacity + alters arm swing in gait. Under athletic load (lifting, sprinting, falling) cervical instability = injury | Tongue-on-roof-of-mouth cue (digastric activation); chin-tucked during efforts; T-spine mobility takes pressure off cervical |
| **Thoracic (T-spine)** | **Extension MOBILITY** specifically | Thoracic stiffness pushes barbells too high during squats (loses leverage) and contributes to kyphotic posture. T-spine extension is the under-trained mobility direction in most modern adults | Daily T-spine extension work; basketball-mobility drill; banded arm circles |
| **Lumbar** | **Stiffness in all directions** | All major back-injury mechanisms involve excess lumbar motion under load — flexion (disc herniation), extension (facet pinching), rotation (annular tearing, ~4× compressive load per equal torque) | Big 3, abdominal brace, hip-hinge for all bends, never twist under load |
| **Sacroiliac (SI)** | **Stability** | SI joint instability is a common cause of low back pain in athletes with hypermobile pelvises. SI joints should not move much | QL + glute stability work; carries; avoid one-leg-deep stretches without control |

## Why the BM "one rule" was insufficient

The coarse rule ("spine stiffness, hips mobility") is correct for the lumbar + SI but WRONG for the T-spine. T-spine wants extension mobility specifically. If you apply "spine = stiffness" globally and never mobilise the T-spine, the body compensates by:
- Cervical hypermobility (chronic neck strain)
- Lumbar hypermobility (the lumbar over-extends to compensate for stiff T-spine, putting more rotation/extension stress on lumbar facets)
- Inability to overhead press cleanly (forces lumbar to extend instead of T-spine)
- Inability to carry a bar high in the squat without flexing the lumbar

## What this changes practically

**Application:**

1. **T-spine extension mobility is a daily target.** If basketball-mobility only lives in shoulder warmups, elevate it to a daily 60-second drill (with travolta-peanuts on alternating days for variety) — alongside the Big 3.

2. **Lumbar stays stiff (the Big 3 protocol stands).** No lumbar mobility work. No flexion-extension cycles beyond cat-camel as warmup. The Big 3 builds the stiffness.

3. **Cervical: tongue-on-roof + chin-tucked during efforts.** UBF's curl-up neck trick generalises: any time you're under load, tongue-to-roof activates the digastric/anterior neck muscles and reduces SCM dominance → less neck strain, better posture.

4. **SI: implicit in the glute + QL training.** Strong glute med + QL → stable SI. No additional SI-specific work needed.

## Why each segment is what it is (anatomical reasoning)

- **Cervical:** small vertebrae, large ranges of motion, vertebral arteries pass through them — high stability demand under load. The "neutral neck during load" rule.
- **Thoracic:** ribcage attached → naturally stiff. Modern sitting + tech use → kyphotic. Extension mobility = the COMPENSATORY work needed to restore baseline function.
- **Lumbar:** 5 large vertebrae bear most spinal load. The discs here are the ones that herniate. Annular collagen layers delaminate with repeated bending. The compressive-shear-twist combo at the L4-L5 / L5-S1 joints is the dominant injury mechanism.
- **Sacroiliac:** designed for very limited motion (1-3 mm typically). Force transmission from leg → pelvis → spine relies on SI stability.

## The hip-and-shoulder mobility complement

UBF's broader rule still holds (extending the lumbar / SI segments above):
- **Hips:** want MOBILITY in all planes — flexion, extension, abduction, external rotation, internal rotation. The hip is built for motion. Stiff hips → spine compensates → injury cascade.
- **Shoulders:** want MOBILITY at the glenohumeral joint, STABILITY at the scapulothoracic joint (scapulae packed and controlled, not floating wildly). The scapular control concept ([protraction-retraction-elevation-depression-scapular-control](protraction-retraction-elevation-depression-scapular-control.md)).
- **Ankles:** want dorsiflexion mobility specifically (most adults are dorsiflexion-limited from shoes). Restricted ankle dorsiflexion = squat depth limited → lumbar flexion compensation → injury.

## Decision tool: "is this a stiffness target or a mobility target?"

Before adding any drill to your routine, ask: which segment is it for?

| Drill examples | Target segment | Goal |
|---|---|---|
| McGill Big 3 | Lumbar | Stiffness |
| Cat-camel (mild, 7-8 cycles) | Whole spine | Lubrication (not stretching) |
| Basketball-mobility | T-spine | Extension mobility |
| Travolta-peanuts | T-spine | Extension mobility |
| Hip airplane | Hip | Mobility + glute activation |
| Pigeon stretch | Hip (external rotation) | Mobility |
| Banded arm circles | Shoulder (GH joint) | Mobility |
| Band pull-aparts | Shoulder (scap retractors) | Stability |
| Suitcase carry | SI + lumbar | Stability under asymmetric load |
| Russian twist | Lumbar | **MOTION (banned)** — wrong target |

If a drill's target conflicts with the segment's wanted state (e.g. lumbar mobility drills), drop the drill.

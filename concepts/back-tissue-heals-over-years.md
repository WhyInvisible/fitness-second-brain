# Back tissue heals over years, not weeks

Per McGill Ch 1 (myth-bust: "Backs can be rehabilitated in 6–12 weeks") + Ch 2 (the "degenerative cascade"). Contrary to the insurance-company assumption — and contrary to the way a broken leg or torn muscle heals — disc and endplate injuries can take **up to 10 years** for the tissue to fully gristle.

This is NOT a sentence of 10 years of pain. It's 10 years of **load management** during which the tissue stabilises and the pain progressively fades. Patients who follow the McGill spine-hygiene protocol typically become pain-free in months to a year while the underlying tissue continues to remodel in the background for years more.

## The mechanism — why so slow

- Discs have very limited blood supply → slow nutrient delivery → slow repair.
- Healing happens through annular collagen re-organisation and the disc gradually stiffening (the "gristling" McGill describes).
- Each re-injury (each "pick the scab" event) resets the clock partially.
- Concurrent cascade: original injury → flattened disc → narrowed joint space → facet joint extra load → facet arthritis over ~2 years → further nerve impingement.

## The implication for asymptomatic patients with injury history

If you've had a back injury and are currently pain-free, the tissue is still healing. You are in the **management window**, not a "cured" state. Behaviours during this window determine whether healing continues or whether you re-injure.

Example: an athlete whose back injury was 5–7 years ago is plausibly past the worst of the cascade but still well within the management window. The next 3–5 years of load management determine whether they reach "fully gristled, robust, low-risk" status or whether a careless heavy session resets the clock. Record the athlete's own back history in [profile.md](../profile.md) → Injuries and niggles.

## Application

For an athlete with back-injury history:

1. **Trap-bar over conventional deadlift is not temporary** — keep it for at least 3–5 years, until the tissue is fully consolidated. Possibly for good.
2. **Big 3 daily is not a "rehab phase" — it's life.** McGill explicitly says (p. 153) "the truth is that most patients will experience a minor relapse at some point in their recovery."
3. **Spine hygiene is not a phase — it's a permanent practice.** Office sitting habits, mattress choice, how the gym bag gets picked up — every day for the next decade.
4. **Any back niggle triggers the Virtual Surgery protocol** ([virtual-surgery-relapse-protocol](virtual-surgery-relapse-protocol.md)), not "rest it for a few days and see."
5. **Plan for the long game.** "I'm pain-free now, so I can go back to maximal deadlifts" is the trap. The better frame: "I'm partway through a 10-year management window. Stay disciplined. The reward is robust health for good."

## What McGill says explicitly

- Human back-injury research shows recovery often needs an extensive set of measures, and true healing can take up to 10 years. That doesn't mean 10 more years of pain; it means managing how the back is loaded over a long period to stay pain-free. (Paraphrased from McGill, *Back Mechanic*, Ch 1–2.)
- Successful patients manage their pain so it doesn't worsen. If a joint injury is properly managed over roughly ten years, the degenerative cascade still runs its course but the pain stays controlled; eventually the joint stiffens on its own and the pain subsides. (Paraphrased from McGill, *Back Mechanic*, Ch 1–2.)

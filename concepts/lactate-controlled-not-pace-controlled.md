# Lactate-controlled, not pace-controlled

When the watch says you're going slower than "you should," trust the body. Most amateurs ruin their threshold sessions by chasing a pace number when the body would have produced more adaptation at 5-10 sec/km slower. RPE + HR over pace.

## Generalised form (Fitzgerald)

The Norwegian principle ("lactate over pace") generalises to a broader rule for non-lab-equipped runners: **HR governs easy and threshold; pace governs VO2max and speed; RPE overrides both on flat days.** See [hr-governs-easy-pace-governs-hard](hr-governs-easy-pace-governs-hard.md). The shared idea: don't let the watch's pace number override the physiological reality of what your body can productively do today.

## Example
In the build-up to a PB, Marius Bakken ran 1000 m repeats at 2:53/km -- well slower than 5k race pace for a 13:06 runner -- to stay at 3 mmol/L. The lactate target took priority over the pace target. Result: a 5000 m national record (13:06).

## Why this matters
A "threshold" workout done 10 sec/km too fast is no longer at threshold -- it's at VO2max territory. The adaptation target shifts, recovery cost spikes, the next session suffers. Same pace label, different stimulus.

## How to apply without a lactate meter
- The "speech in short full sentences" test for LT1 -- if you can speak in 4-word phrases but not full sentences, you're between LT1 and LT2.
- Use HR as the constraint; if HR climbs above target, slow down even if pace feels easy.
- End the interval if form breaks or breathing becomes ragged.

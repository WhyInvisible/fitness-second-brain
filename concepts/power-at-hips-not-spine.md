# Power belongs at the hips and shoulders — never the spine

Per McGill Ch 1, Power = velocity × force. Generating high power AT the spine — either bending fast or bending under load or both — is the dominant catastrophic injury mechanism for the back. The remedy is structural: route ALL power through the ball-and-socket joints (hips, shoulders), keeping the spine locked in neutral as the force-transmitting pillar.

## The biomechanical case

The spine is a flexible rod, not a power-generating mechanism. Discs deform under bending; repeated bending (especially under load or at speed) delaminates collagen layers and herniates nuclei. The hips and shoulders are ball-and-socket joints DESIGNED to move under load — they have synovial capsules, deep articulation, and surrounding musculature built for power production.

Therefore:
- An Olympic clean-and-jerk pulls power from the hip drive, with a locked spine.
- A baseball pitcher generates power from the hips, transmits through a stiffened core, releases at the shoulder.
- A boxer's punch is hip-drive + locked spine + shoulder rotation.

When power generation moves INTO the spine — the powerlifter who flexion-grinds a deadlift, the bodybuilder who arches under a squat, the golfer who twists the lumbar instead of the hip on backswing — the spine takes the load it was never built for. Injury follows.

## What a typical hinge injury looks like

The classic heavy-deadlift back injury is a power-at-the-spine event: the rep where hip drive runs out, the back muscles take over, the spine bends under load, the disc delaminates. Power must come from the hips at all times during a hinge. The moment hip drive ends, the rep ends — no grinding through with back muscle. If the athlete's back history in [profile.md](../profile.md) includes an injury like this, everything below applies with extra force.

## Practical consequences

- **Trap-bar > conventional barbell deadlift** for an athlete with a back history — trap-bar puts the load axis closer to the hip and reduces shear on the lumbar. Hip drive can dominate.
- **Hip thrust is high-value** — pure hip power generator, spine in neutral against a bench, glutes drive the load. Dicharry's 2× BW target ([runner-strength-loading-targets](runner-strength-loading-targets.md)) is appropriate here.
- **Kettlebell swing requires assessment** — IF technique is hip-drive-and-locked-spine, it's gold. IF it becomes a spine-flex-extension oscillation, it's catastrophic for someone with back history. McGill explicitly warns spondylolisthesis patients off KB swings. An athlete with back history should learn it under coaching first.
- **Back squat is acceptable IF** technique stays vertical-torso + hip-hinge entry. Goblet / front squat are lower-risk substitutes.
- **NEVER program** seated rotation machines, Russian twists, supermans, or any "power at spine" exercise.
- **Plyometrics rule** — Dicharry's "ground time, not jump height" + landing with locked spine ([plyometrics-ground-time-not-jump-height](plyometrics-ground-time-not-jump-height.md)) is consistent with this principle.

## The McGill line that anchors this

McGill's framing, paraphrased: power is force times velocity, so "power at the spine" means bending or moving the spine forcefully, and producing power that way is a major injury risk (McGill, *Back Mechanic*, Ch 1).

## Pavel's cross-source agreement

Pavel cites McGill on this directly (p. 56, with a photo of the two together) and operationalises it in the kettlebell swing's mechanical demands:
- **Hike-pass entry** — the bell is hiked between the legs with the hips fully loaded; the spine is locked neutral throughout. See [hip-hinge-mechanics](hip-hinge-mechanics.md).
- **Plank at the top** — at the apex of the swing, the body forms a straight line: knees locked, glutes cramped, abs braced, lats engaged, spine neutral. "Do not lean back at the top" — the back doing the glute's job is the dominant injury mechanism.
- **Anti-shrug via lats** ("the super-muscles") — pushing the shoulders DOWN via lat engagement keeps load transmitting down through the body instead of compressing the cervical spine.
- **"Release the bell if heels lift"** — when balance starts to demand spine compensation, abandon the rep. "Save your back to swing another day."

The cross-source agreement gives this principle TWO independent biomechanical authorities (McGill the academic + Pavel the field-trained Soviet practitioner). For an athlete with a back-injury history, this concept is THE most-strongly-evidenced principle in the workspace.

Pavel adds the *positive* version of the principle: where you SHOULD put the power (the hips, with maximal hardstyle explosive intent during the contraction window — see [hardstyle-tension-relaxation](hardstyle-tension-relaxation.md)). McGill only tells you where NOT to.

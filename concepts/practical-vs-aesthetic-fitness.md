# Practical physical capability is the real metric, not "fitness"

Per Le Corre Manifesto principle 4 ("Practical") + principle 5 ("Vital"), the goal of physical training should be *real-world capability* — what you can DO in the environments and situations life actually presents — not what numbers you hit in a controlled gym setting or what you look like in a mirror.

> "Few people actually want to be fit; most just want to look fit." — Le Corre, *The Practice of Natural Movement*

In other words, most people chase the image of fitness rather than its function.

## The two frames

| Aesthetic / metric fitness | Practical physical capability |
|---|---|
| Numbers on a barbell | Can lift the object that needs lifting |
| Race times + Strava QOMs | Can run / walk far enough for the day's task |
| Body-fat percentage | Body that responds well to environmental demands |
| Visible muscle definition | Functional muscle in the patterns life demands |
| Tested in controlled environments | Tested in real environments with real consequences |
| Single-discipline specialisation | Broad vocabulary across ~10–12 skills |
| Done alone, mostly seated on machines | Often cooperative (lift / carry / throw together) |
| Easy to quantify, harder to test | Hard to quantify, easy to test (just go DO things) |

## Why the practical frame matters

1. **The aesthetic frame is the dominant failure mode of modern fitness.** Le Corre's cultural critique is unrhetorical: most people training in commercial gyms are pursuing image, not function. Net result: bodybuilder-physiques that can't climb a tree, marathoners with no upper-body strength, lifters who can't run, yogis who can't lift, etc.
2. **Capability transfers to life; aesthetic doesn't.** When a friend needs help moving, when a kid needs catching, when you need to climb something quickly, when you need to walk for miles on uneven trail — the question is *can you do it well, safely, repeatedly*, not what your bench-press 1RM is.
3. **The aesthetic frame breaks under adversity.** Aesthetic fitness depends on controlled conditions (gym, equipment, regimen, sleep, food, mirror). Practical capability degrades more slowly when conditions shift (travel, illness, no-equipment, varied environment).
4. **Capability is testable across discipline lines; aesthetic is not.** A 1:30 half-marathoner with no pull-ups, no carry strength, and no balance is *narrow*. A 1:30 half-marathoner who can carry a 50 kg pack 5 km, hang for 60 s, climb a wall, and balance-walk a fallen log is *broad*.

## What changes in practice if you adopt this frame

- **Goal-setting** stops being purely metric-based (race times, lifts, body weight) and adds capability goals (carry X for Y minutes, climb Z, balance-walk a 4 m beam at 3 ft, hang 90 s, sprint 200 m and immediately precision-jump 2 m forward + stick the landing).
- **Training environments** widen: outdoor + uneven + varied + cooperative becomes equal-value to gym + flat + predictable + solo.
- **Discipline boundaries** soften: instead of "running day vs strength day vs yoga day," more sessions integrate carrying + balancing + climbing alongside the headline focus.
- **Cooperation** becomes a normal training mode (partner carries, throw/catch, climbing with a spotter), not just a motivation hack.
- **Travel** stops being a "training deficit period" and becomes a *natural-movement laboratory*.

## Hébert's motto

Le Corre cites Georges Hébert's lineage motto repeatedly: **"Be strong to be useful"** — both to oneself and to others. The framing is moral as well as practical: physical capability is *for someone*, not just *of someone*.

## Application

- **If a marathon is the A-race, it is correctly prioritised until race day** (per [marathon-goal-wins-conflicts](marathon-goal-wins-conflicts.md)) — that's a metric goal but a legitimate one. The aesthetic-vs-practical frame doesn't argue against marathon training; it argues against marathon training as a *permanent identity*.
- **Add an annual practical-capability dashboard** alongside the race-time and body-weight goals in [goals.md](../goals.md). Example targets: carry 50 kg over 100 m on uneven ground, hang 60 s, climb a 3 m wall, jump and stick a 1.8 m horizontal precision jump, balance-walk a 4 m beam at 3 ft. These force the broader vocabulary and prevent post-race drift into "the next race" without a wider physical identity.
- **The off-season after the A-race** is the natural moment to test this frame. Calisthenics goals (handstand, muscle-up) are *partially* practical-capability targets (the handstand is mostly aesthetic; the muscle-up is high practical value as the "exit climb at top of wall" gate). Add 1–2 broader-capability goals to balance the calisthenics specificity.
- **Travel periods** are the ideal capability-test period: no gym, varied terrain, real environments — exactly where practical capability is differentiable from metric fitness. Use a trip as a *test* of capability, not just a maintenance phase.

## What this frame does NOT say

- It does NOT say metric goals are bad. Marathon times, lifts, body weight matter. The argument is they shouldn't be the *only* metrics.
- It does NOT say specialisation is bad. Specialising in a discipline for a phase produces real gains. The argument is against *permanent* specialisation as an identity.
- It does NOT say outdoor / "primal" is required. Le Corre is explicit: indoor controlled environments are useful for safety + scalability + technique acquisition. The argument is against *only* indoor controlled environments.
- It does NOT preach asceticism. Le Corre's lifestyle critiques (sitting all day, screens, processed food) are real, but the stance is anti-*deficit*, not anti-modern.

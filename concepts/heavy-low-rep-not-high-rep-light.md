# Heavy + low rep, not high rep + light

3-5 reps at hard weights builds force without adding mass or excess fatigue. High-rep work just adds soreness that compromises running.

## Why for runners

- Force production (not size) = better running economy
- Low reps = high CNS demand but low metabolic cost = doesn't compromise next day's run
- High reps to failure = hypertrophy stimulus = unwanted mass + soreness

## Where the sources diverge on rep range

This concept is one of the most contested in the strength literature. Different rep ranges for different goals:

| Source | Rep range | Goal | Use case |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Runner default** | 3-5 | Force, low CNS cost, no mass | Marathon-block lifting |
| **Dicharry** ([runner-strength-loading-targets](runner-strength-loading-targets.md)) | 6-8 | Force at BW-multiple targets | Runner-strength block, post-marathon |
| **Matthews / BLS** | 4-6 | Hypertrophy + strength (natural lifters) | Bodybuilding, off-season mass block |
| **Pfitzinger** | 15 (light, circuit) | "Force without mass" via reps | Light maintenance, travel weeks |

Matthews explicitly argues AGAINST 8-10 rep "hypertrophy" ranges for natural lifters, calling 4-6 "the sweet spot" for both strength and size. That's right *for the BLS goal* (mass + strength in a bodybuilder).

**In a marathon block, go LOWER than Matthews (3-5)** because the aim is force-only adaptation with minimal metabolic / soreness cost. The lower the rep range, the closer to pure-strength adaptation and the further from hypertrophy.

**In an off-season block, move to Matthews' 4-6 range** if building some mass is a legitimate goal in [goals.md](../goals.md) (calisthenics skills like muscle-up + L-sit benefit from upper-body mass; pistol squat benefits from quad mass).

## Why high reps to failure is always wrong for a runner

- Triggers hypertrophy + maximum CNS / metabolic fatigue
- Produces DOMS that compromises next day's run
- Doesn't build force production (the actual goal)
- Adds visible mass (counterproductive for endurance — more bodyweight to carry over 42 km)
- Trains a poor motor pattern (grinding through fatigue)

## The unifying principle across sources

Whatever the rep range:
- **Use enough load that the prescribed rep count is challenging** (Matthews: 80-85% 1RM at 4-6; the runner default of 3-5 = 85-90% 1RM)
- **Stop at the prescribed rep count, NOT at failure** — leave 2-3 reps in the tank
- **Apply [bar-speed-rule](bar-speed-rule.md)** — set ends when speed crashes >50%
- **Apply [double-progression-loading-scheme](double-progression-loading-scheme.md)** — beat last week by 1 rep, hit top of range → add weight

## Pfitzinger circuit is a different category

Pfitzinger prescribes 2× weekly light-weight, 15-rep, 8-exercise full-body circuits as an EXPLICIT runner-strength tool. This isn't "high-rep failure work" — it's circuit-style maintenance with minimal CNS load. Different mechanism: metabolic + connective-tissue adaptation, not force production. Both can be programmed; use the circuit during travel weeks (bodyweight-friendly when there's no gym) or as the maintenance option in the marathon-build maintenance phase.

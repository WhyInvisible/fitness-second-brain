# Environmental adaptability is fitness — train across varied contexts

Per Le Corre Manifesto principles 7 (Adaptable) + 8 (Environmental) + 11 (Mindful), the **complexity of the environment you train in** is a first-class training variable — equal to volume and intensity. Training only in controlled, predictable environments (flat gym floor, rubber surface, identical bar) produces narrow capability that doesn't transfer to the real world's uneven / unstable / variable / unpredictable contexts.

Le Corre's point on mindfulness: meditation has a place in the practice, but movement is not meditation with the eyes shut — while moving, attention has to stay open to both what the body is doing and what the environment is presenting (Le Corre, *The Practice of Natural Movement*, Pt 1, Manifesto principle 11).

## The VIC framework — complexity as the under-used axis

Le Corre's progression framework: **Volume × Intensity × Complexity**.

- **Volume** — reps, duration. Develops endurance + technique consolidation.
- **Intensity** — speed, weight, distance, height, force-of-impact. Develops strength + power + work capacity.
- **Complexity** — environmental + situational variables. Develops adaptability + balance + decision-making + real-world transfer.

Conventional fitness modulates only V + I. Le Corre's argument: *complexity is the under-used axis with the highest neural-adaptation payoff*. Same exercise, harder context → bigger brain-body adaptation, often more useful than just adding weight or reps.

**Example:** 10 depth jumps from 3 ft height landing on a 1 ft × 1 ft thin board (small landing target) vs the same 10 depth jumps landing on the flat floor. Identical V + I; the board adds complexity. The board version develops accuracy + landing precision + spatial awareness; the flat version doesn't.

## Controlled vs uncontrolled environments

| Variable | Controlled (custom indoor) | Uncontrolled (nature, urban) |
|---|---|---|
| Variety | Built-in by design | High (when present) or absent |
| Predictability | High | Low |
| Scalability | High (adjustable equipment) | Low (you take what's there) |
| Safety | High | Lower |
| Quantification | Easy | Hard |
| Mental engagement required | Variable (can drift) | Forced (consequences are real) |
| Air quality | Often poor (rubber, plastic) | Usually better |
| Cost of access | Often paid | Free |
| Repeatable progression | Yes | No |

**Le Corre's recommendation:** use *both*, not one. Controlled for skill acquisition (safety + scalability); uncontrolled for capability testing + mental challenge + the irreplaceable benefits of nature.

## The Alloway working-memory study

Le Corre cites the only published study on MovNat-style training: subjects who did balance-on-beam + climb-a-tree showed a ~50% boost in working memory after a single session. Yoga group: no change. Sedentary control: no change.

The mechanism: *mind-body-context* engagement (Le Corre's term) demands cognitive interaction with external complexity — far more brain-loading than mind-body alone (yoga, meditation) or body-only (treadmill). Working memory is more important than IQ in predicting real-world performance. Therefore: complex-environment movement training is high-leverage cognitive training too.

## Why this matters operationally

1. **Training "in your head" is a real failure mode.** Treadmill running, gym lifting, indoor cycling — done with phone / music / TV / podcast — produces movement but not adaptability. The complexity dimension is zero; the cognitive engagement is zero.
2. **Adaptability requires *exposure to variability*, not just willingness to adapt.** You cannot train adaptability with the intention to adapt; you train it by encountering variables that demand actual adaptation.
3. **Real-world demands are mostly complexity-bound, not intensity-bound.** Most situations where someone "needs to be fit" (carrying a child up stairs, helping move furniture, hiking with a pack, escaping danger) are moderate intensity but high complexity. Training for max intensity alone misses this.
4. **Risk-to-danger ratio is the safety calibration.** Le Corre's specific framework: scale complexity to environments where the *danger* (consequence of failure) is low enough to permit the *risk* (probability of failure) inherent in learning. Beginner balance work on a 2x4 on the floor = high risk (will fall off), zero danger (just step off). Same skill at 30 ft elevation = same risk, lethal danger. Wrong order.

## Application

- **During a marathon build:** the easiest complexity additions with near-zero recovery cost are: (a) one weekly trail run (uneven terrain → balance, ankle-strength, stepping accuracy) instead of pure road, (b) one weekly outdoor session at a park instead of indoor gym, (c) using a balance beam (2x4 on the floor) for 5 min daily warm-ups, (d) one weekly carry session on uneven outdoor ground (grass, hill, dirt) rather than flat indoor floor. None of these add intensity; all add complexity. See exercises/balance-beam-walk, exercises/quadrupedal-crawl.

- **Travel / no-gym phases:** the entire training context becomes uncontrolled. This is *the* opportunity to train complexity at scale — treat such weeks as the dedicated complexity-training period of the year. See the travel template in [MOCs/MOC-natural-movement.md](../MOCs/MOC-natural-movement.md).

- **Off-season (after the A-race):** add a weekly outdoor "course" session — a multi-skill flow over varied terrain (balance + crawl + climb + carry + jump) — alongside the indoor calisthenics block.

- **Mental coupling:** Le Corre's mindfulness principle (no phone, no music, full attention to environment) couples directly. Treat outdoor / complex-environment sessions as *attention-training* too. This is a credible counter to a multi-discipline athlete's cognitive overload — focused-attention practice with metabolic + physical benefits.

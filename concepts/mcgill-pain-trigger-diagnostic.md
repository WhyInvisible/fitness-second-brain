# McGill's pain-trigger diagnostic framework

Per McGill Ch 5–6, "non-specific back pain" is a clinical cop-out — every back pain has a specific mechanical trigger. The job is to find it via structured self-assessment, then design around it.

## The taxonomy

Every back pain falls into ONE OR MORE of these intolerance categories. Each has a different "relief" and a different "avoid":

| Category | Trigger | Probable cause | Relief | Avoid |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Flexion-intolerant** | Slouched sitting, bending, picking up | Disc bulge (most common) | Tummy lie + fist under chin | Knees-to-chest, slouched sitting, sit-ups |
| **Extension-intolerant** | Arching back, lying flat on stiff bed | Facet joint, spondylolisthesis | Slight flexion, neutral spine | Cobra pose, deep back extensions |
| **Compression-intolerant** | Holding loads in front, opening windows | Endplate damage | Spine hygiene, reduced load | Forward reaches, holding loads at distance |
| **Dynamic-load-intolerant** | Stairs, running, uneven ground | Compressive endplate | Abdominal brace OR pec-lat brace | Vibration, jumping, jogging until rebuilt |
| **Twist-intolerant** | Rotating under load | Disc tear from twist + bend | Move from hips, stop-twist tool | Russian twists, golf swing |
| **Episodic** | Pain-free windows interrupted by attacks | Disc bulge with intermittent contact on nerve | Log, identify recurring trigger | Re-introducing the trigger after a pain-free window |
| **Walking-distance limited** | Cumulative — fine for first 5 min, then pain | Stenosis (older), facet wind-up | Park-bench decompression, interval walking | Pushing through |
| **Neural tension** | Pain radiating to leg/foot, worse with neck flex | Disc bulge pinching nerve root | Tummy lie, nerve flossing | End-range stretching of any kind |

## How to identify your category — the 8 tests

(Full descriptions in references/mcgill-back-mechanic.md. Run them in order. Log everything.)

1. **Seated compression test** (4 positions: neutral, flexed, extended, plus neck variants)
2. **Heel drop** — with and without abdominal brace / pec-lat brace
3. **Tummy lie** — 3 minutes, does it relieve or worsen?
4. **Standing extension + twist** — and does single-leg stance change it?
5. **Wall plank** — cycle through lumbar + hip ranges
6. **Load tolerance** — 5 lb at belt buckle, then arms outstretched
7. **Pelvic motion under unloaded bar** — does flex/extend trigger?
8. **Standing posture** — palpate low back muscles, find the position that lets them relax

## The two action lists

Before the tests, McGill asks you to write down:
- List A: Activities that increase your back pain today
- List B: Activities that DO NOT increase it (or that you can do pain-free)

Then look for the common feature across List A. It usually maps to one of the categories above.

## Why this matters even when pain-free

For an athlete who is currently pain-free, this isn't an active diagnostic. BUT:
- If a back niggle EVER returns, this is the protocol to run that same day — before assuming "rest will fix it" or "go see a physio".
- Knowing the framework lets the athlete communicate with any clinician in McGill's vocabulary (and bring the book to the appointment, as McGill suggests).
- The diagnostic also DISCRIMINATES — a sharp stab when sneezing is different from a creeping ache after long sitting is different from leg numbness during long walks. Each suggests different causes and different responses.

## Using injury history to guess a likely category

If the athlete has a past back injury (see [profile.md](../profile.md) → Injuries and niggles), the mechanism is the best first clue. A heavy-deadlift injury is almost always **flexion + load** — the canonical disc-bulge mechanism (spine bent forward under high compression) — and leaves a likely latent flexion intolerance. In that case:
- Bent-over rows could be a hidden trigger
- Slouched desk work is a long-term risk
- Sit-ups would be catastrophic
- Hip-hinge mechanics are non-negotiable
- Trap-bar > conventional barbell

Even with zero current pain, treating that athlete as flexion-intolerant by default is the safe assumption.

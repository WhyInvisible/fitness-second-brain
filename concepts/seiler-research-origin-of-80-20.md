# Seiler's research and the origin of 80/20

The 80/20 Rule wasn't designed — it was *measured*. Stephen Seiler, an American exercise physiologist working at Agder University in Kristiansand, Norway, spent the early-to-mid 2000s painstakingly auditing the training of elite endurance athletes and discovering that they had independently converged on the same pattern: ~80% of training time at low intensity, ~20% at high intensity, with the middle deliberately avoided.

## The trigger observation

In Norway, Seiler watched Olympic-caliber cross-country skiers *walking up steep hills* to keep heart rate from creeping above the low-intensity zone. Seiler called this "excellent intensity discipline" — the willingness to slow down dramatically rather than let a workout drift. This was the opposite of what most amateurs do.

## The measurement studies

- **Elite junior XC skiers** — 75% of workouts entirely low-intensity; **91% of total time** below VT.
- **Norwegian rowers 1970–2001** — over 30 years, the share of low-intensity training rose 67% while moderate/high-intensity training fell ~60%. Performance (6-min ergometer trial) rose 10% over the same period. By 2001: ~88/12 ratio.
- **Elite swimmers (Mujika 2003)** — 77% low intensity, 23% mod/high.
- **Elite cyclists (Zapico 2007)** — 78% low intensity; the period when they dropped to 70% saw zero improvement.
- **Elite triathlete (Mujika 2012, Olympic-bound)** — 74–88% low intensity across the three disciplines.
- **Elite runners (Billat 2001 — French + Portuguese marathoners; Billat 2003 — Kenyans)** — 78–85% below LT.

Pattern: independent of sport, country, era, the elite end converges on ~80/20.

## The controlled trials

With the observational case made, Seiler and collaborator **Jonathan Esteve-Lanao** (Spanish exercise scientist + coach) ran randomised comparisons:

1. **Esteve-Lanao & Seiler 2007** — 12 sub-elite runners, 5 months: 80/20 vs 65/35 at matched training load. The 80/20 group improved 10K time by **2:37 (7%)**; the 65/35 group by 2:01 (5.3%). The 30% larger improvement was statistically significant.
2. **Esteve-Lanao & Seiler 2013** — 30 recreational runners (10K ~39 min) on 80/20 vs ~50/50. The 80/20 group lowered 10K time by 1:59 (5%); the 50/50 group by 1:24 (3.5%). Among the most compliant 80/20 subjects (actual 78% low intensity), improvement reached 7%.
3. **Stöggl & Sperlich 2014 (Salzburg)** — 48 athletes × 4 sports × 4 protocols × 9 weeks. The polarised protocol (68% low / 6% moderate / 26% high) won: time-to-exhaustion +17.4%, VO2max +11.7%. The threshold-dominant protocol (46/54/0) saw VO2max *decline* 4.1%.

## Why this matters

Before Seiler, the consensus in exercise physiology favoured **high-intensity interval training** for fitness — based on short-duration controlled trials with non-athletes. Seiler's contribution: real elite athletes training over months and years show the opposite pattern, and matched-load randomised trials with real athletes confirm 80/20 wins. The HIIT-skewed lab literature is misleading when extrapolated to endurance training.

## Why the elites converged blindly

Fitzgerald argues that the elites reaching 80/20 without knowing it is itself strong evidence that the ratio is optimal: "that's how evolution works — solving problems blindly" (Fitzgerald, *80/20 Running*, Ch 3). Bill Rodgers' 1975 Boston-build week was 81% easy with no ratio in mind. Mo Farah's contemporary week is ~80%, also not programmed as a ratio. They just trained.

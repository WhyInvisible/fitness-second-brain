# Flexibility ≠ mobility -- three causes of stiffness, each with a different fix

Per Dicharry Ch 3, "stiffness" isn't one problem -- it's at least three, each with a different fix. Using the wrong tool usually makes it worse.

## McGill's overlay — applies to MOBILITY joints, not the spine

Per McGill Ch 2: this whole framework applies to joints DESIGNED for mobility (hips, ankles, T-spine, shoulders). For the LUMBAR SPINE, the goal is the opposite — stiffness, not flexibility. Stretching a back that hurts is almost never therapeutic and is often actively harmful (triggers stretch reflex → 15 min false relief → tissue worsens). See [spine-stiffness-not-flexibility](spine-stiffness-not-flexibility.md). Practical: apply Dicharry's framework to hips / hamstrings / T-spine; apply McGill's "stop stretching, start stiffening" rule to the lumbar.

## The three causes

### 1. Blocked joint (doorstop in the hinge)
- Symptom: ROM is hard-stopped at a particular point, not gradually tight
- Fix: joint glide work (e.g. ankle belt mobilisation), NOT stretching
- Stretching a blocked joint = forcing through impingement = worsens it

### 2. Short tissue (actually too short)
- Symptom: gradual tightness through the range, can be lengthened
- Fix: **3-minute static stretches, 4-5x/wk for 10 weeks** to actually lengthen
- Short sessions, sporadic frequency = no real length change

### 3. Fascial restriction (post-overload)
- Symptom: feels "knotted", localised tightness after hard training
- Fix: foam rolling / massage / self-myofascial release
- Stretching doesn't address fascial adhesions

### Plus: cold tissue
- Not actually stiff, just not warmed up
- Fix: dynamic warm-up (neural reset, not length change)
- Static stretching cold = mild risk, no benefit

## How to know which one
Test the range with patience. Hard stop = joint. Gradual = tissue. Localised + post-workout = fascia. Pre-workout + warms out = cold.

# Your body drives your form

Mobility / stability / wiring gaps cap how well you can execute *any* form cue. Telling yourself "land closer to your body" or "fire your glutes" without first fixing the upstream body issue is a waste of breath -- the muscle memory doesn't exist to draw on.

Per Dicharry 2017.

## Diagnose. Then cue. Then practice.
1. Assess movement quality (squat depth, single-leg balance, T-spine rotation)
2. Identify the upstream limiter (tight hip flexors, weak glute med, blocked ankle dorsiflexion, etc.)
3. Fix the limiter (the right tool for the right problem -- see [flexibility-vs-mobility-three-stiffness-causes](flexibility-vs-mobility-three-stiffness-causes.md))
4. Re-test the form cue
5. THEN practice the cue

## Application
A classic example is late-race cramping in a marathon. If the athlete's legs and posterior chain are undertrained, cramping in the final 10 km is often an upstream-body problem rather than a willpower or fuelling problem (see [cramping-is-neuromuscular-fatigue](cramping-is-neuromuscular-fatigue.md)). The cue "don't cramp" or "hold form" is meaningless without the leg strength to back it, so the fix is strength work in the build, not a better cue on the day.

## McGill's parallel framing

McGill arrives at the same principle from the back-safety angle: pain alters movement engrams (the spinal-cord-encoded motor patterns). Form fixes that ignore the upstream body issue — pain, mobility block, weak glutes, tight hip flexors — just train the body to repeat the dysfunction. Both authors agree: assess body, fix body, then train form.
